\chapter{Literature Review}

Two lines of work are relevant to this study. The first estimates ground-level \pmtf{} from
satellite and auxiliary data, so that concentrations can be mapped where no monitor exists. The
second forecasts \pmtf{} hours to days ahead at monitored sites, usually with deep spatiotemporal
networks. The two answer different questions, and the difference matters when their reported
accuracies are compared.

\section{Satellite-based estimation of ground-level PM2.5}

The most direct use of AOD is to scale it to surface concentration. Dey et al.\ \cite{dey2020india}
built a 1\,km daily \pmtf{} record for India from 2000 to 2019 by converting MAIAC AOD with a
time-varying scaling factor taken from MERRA-2. Against CPCB monitors the daily estimates reached
\rsq{} 0.8 (RMSE 25.7\ugm{}), and annual means 0.97. The method depends on AOD being available, so
days without a retrieval have to be filled in some other way.

Gap filling is a recurring theme. Maheshwarkar and Sunder Raman \cite{maheshwarkar2021central}
estimated daily \pmtf{} over Madhya Pradesh for 2018--2019. They first filled missing MAIAC AOD with
values derived from MERRA-2 and then applied a three-stage statistical model combining a linear
mixed-effects model with geographically weighted regression. Cross-validated $r^2$ ranged from
0.36 to 0.69, a reminder that accuracy varies strongly between places and periods even within one
state.

Machine-learning models are now the most common choice. Wei et al.\ \cite{wei2020stet} proposed a
space--time extremely randomized trees model that combines 1\,km MAIAC AOD with meteorology, land
use, topography and population, and reported an out-of-sample cross-validated \rsq{} of 0.89
(RMSE 10.3\ugm{}) over China. Kawano et al.\ \cite{kawano2025india} produced daily 10\,km estimates
for India from 2005 to 2023 with two stages: AOD gaps were first imputed with machine learning,
and an extreme gradient boosting model then predicted \pmtf{}. They validated with spatial
cross-validation, holding out groups of monitors rather than random days, and obtained \rsq{} 0.67.
The use of spatial validation is important: random splits let a model see the same station on
neighbouring days during training, which inflates the score for unmonitored locations.

Two studies point to alternatives to AOD. Son et al.\ \cite{son2023tropomi} estimated surface
\pmtf{} over Thailand from five TROPOMI gas products (NO\textsubscript{2}, SO\textsubscript{2},
O\textsubscript{3}, CO and HCHO) without any aerosol optical input, using the TabNet deep-learning
model; carbon monoxide was the most influential input, reflecting seasonal biomass burning.
Ahmad et al.\ \cite{ahmad2025lahore} estimated air quality at unmonitored sensor locations in Lahore
with a graph neural network that combines MAIAC AOD, ERA5 meteorology, road networks, points of
interest, population and green space. Their test used 30 low-cost sensors over twelve days in
December 2024, with two sensors held out per run, and reported only MAE and RMSE; population was the
most useful spatial feature.

\section{Spatiotemporal forecasting of PM2.5}

A second body of work forecasts \pmtf{} at existing monitoring stations. Qi et al.\
\cite{qi2019gclstm} combined a graph convolutional network, which passes information between
stations within 200\,km, with an LSTM for the temporal dynamics (GC-LSTM). With hourly data from 76
stations in the Beijing--Tianjin--Hebei region (January 2015 to April 2016) they reached \rsq{} 0.72
for 72-hour forecasts. Their training, validation and test sets were random 60/20/20 splits of
blocks of time.

Chen et al.\ \cite{chen2025hybrid} built a directed graph whose edges follow wind direction and
distance between 29 cities on the North China Plain, again combined with an LSTM, and trained it on
2019--2021 data with 2023 held out for testing. Their mean absolute error rose from 7.6 to
14.0\ugm{} between 3- and 48-hour forecasts. Ni et al.\ \cite{ni2023asgcn} proposed an adaptive
spatio-temporal graph convolutional network with static and dynamic graphs and tested it on the
KnowAir dataset of 184 Chinese cities and on the UrbanAir dataset. Shi et al.\ \cite{shi2026astgt}
used a graph transformer with adaptive normalisation for non-stationary data, evaluated on a
multi-site Beijing dataset and on an Indian multi-city dataset. Shaban \cite{shaban2026cnnlstm}
combined MAIAC AOD, OpenAQ/WAQI ground data and ERA5 meteorology in a CNN-LSTM model, filling AOD
gaps with an autoencoder, and reported \rsq{} 0.86 for 24-hour forecasts against 0.71 for a plain
LSTM; the cities and dataset sizes used are not described in detail.

All five forecasting studies feed the model the stations' own recent \pmtf{} history. Their high
scores therefore measure how well the near future can be predicted from the recent past at the
same place. They do not measure how well \pmtf{} can be estimated where there is no monitor, and
satellite data play at most a minor role in them.

\section{Research gap}

Four gaps follow from this review.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Estimation studies report how accurate their models are, but not how much accuracy is
        lost when cloud removes the satellite input, nor how often that happens. For a region with
        two monsoons this is the first question to answer.
  \item Few studies evaluate at stations the model has never seen, and those that do (for
        example Kawano et al.\ \cite{kawano2025india}) show that spatial validation gives much lower
        scores than random validation.
  \item No study reviewed here covers South India specifically, or reports Kerala's monsoon, where
        concentrations stay moderate while satellite coverage almost disappears.
  \item The forecasting studies rely on random or single chronological splits and rarely give
        uncertainty intervals, and none isolates how much satellite inputs add to forecasting
        skill by comparing the same model with and without them.
\end{enumerate}

This study addresses these gaps with a two-tier design evaluated under temporal and spatial
validation, block-bootstrap confidence intervals, and an explicit breakdown by cloud fraction,
season and state.
