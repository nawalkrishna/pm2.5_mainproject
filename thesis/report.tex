% --------------- Abstract page -----------------------
\begin{center}
{\Large{\bf{ABSTRACT}}}
\end{center}


Ground monitors measure fine particulate matter (\pmtf{}) accurately but cover only a small part
of South India, and satellites, which see the whole region, cannot see through the cloud of its two
monsoons. This report measures how much cloud and the monsoon degrade satellite-based estimation of
daily ground-level \pmtf{} over Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and how
much a two-tier model recovers. Quality-controlled hourly data from 113 stations for 2021--2025 were
combined with TROPOMI trace gases, MAIAC aerosol optical depth (AOD), ERA5/ERA5-Land meteorology,
MERRA-2 aerosol and static land-surface features. Tier A uses the satellite inputs; Tier B uses
everything else and can be applied on every day. Random Forest models were evaluated under temporal
and spatial validation with block-bootstrap confidence intervals.

Cloud acts mainly by removing satellite coverage: MAIAC AOD exists on 32.4\,\% of valid station-days
at the station pixel, on 11.8\,\% in Kerala's south-west monsoon, and on 9\,\% of days with a cloud
fraction above 0.8. Where it exists, the satellite adds little once reanalysis aerosol and meteorology
are in the model: $+0.020$ in \rsq{} at unseen stations, and $+0.049$ in the south-west monsoon. Tier B
therefore carries almost all of the skill, which at an unseen station is modest (\rsq{} 0.32), and no
model beats a monthly climatology in Kerala's monsoon. In an extension that forecasts hourly \pmtf{}
1--72 hours ahead at existing stations, a Random Forest outperforms an LSTM and a graph neural network
at every horizon (\rsq{} 0.81 at 1 hour, 0.43 at 24 hours), and satellite inputs again add nothing.
In a monsoon region, the satellite-free model is the core of a daily \pmtf{} system.
